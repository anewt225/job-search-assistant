### **New Reality \#1:** Technology and persistent economic trends give employers more power.

### **New Reality \#2:** personal brand isn’t a mere nice-to-have … It’s a necessity. For everyone.

- Nearly half of employers (47%) report that they are less likely to bring in a candidate for interviews if they ‘can’t find’ the candidate online.  
- Looking for: Half say “a professional online persona”

  - Reaching profile completeness (for a *specific job)*

## The Hiring Process, Explained

Hiring process is highly dependent on: relationships, timing, and location.

**Active job seekers** \- Eagerly searching & solidly on the job market: unemployment, underemployment, etc.  
**Passive job seekers** \- not seeking a new job, will only respond to recruiter/manager if timing & role are right  
**Tiptoers** \- somewhere in between the first 2: modestly networking, laying seeds, proactively monitoring

Neither recruiters nor hiring managers want to waste extra cycles digging deeply for the perfect candidate … when a “good-enough”: one is available. … Your goal is to perch atop this list of low-hanging fruit.”

The Hiring process unfolds:

1. Recruiters seek **focused** candidates  
   1. Lowest-risk, lowest-hanging candidates: look, swim and quack like the duck trying to find  
2. They then look for **serious** candidates  
   1. Not knowing about company, being open to opportunities, sparse/outdated profile  
3. Hirers rely on **internal referrals** as *the* crucial source to generate high-quality candidate leads  
   1. Referrals get special consideration  
4. Candidates get chosen for interviews based on both their **connections to the employer** and their **knowledge of the organization**  
   1. Easiest, low-risk hires are internal transfers  
   2. Job seekers looking to break in need to punctuate the inner circle and make themselves appear as low-risk as possible.  
5. Hirers, we all know, do **background checks** on candidates before they extend a job offer  
   1. Not just formal, but “also a continual and less formal undertaking throughout the process.  
   2. **Backchanneling** \- when a recruiter, or more often the hiring manager, reaches out to a shared connection to get intel on a candidate while they’re in the consideration process.  
   3. 93% of recruiters are likely to look at social media profiles (primarily but not only LinkedIn)

### Hacking the Hiring Process

1. Build a **LinkedIn profile that’s laser-focused** on what hirers are looking for  
   1. Since recruiters are risk-averse, you must look like a safe choice. They want you to have the right background, titles, experience, connections, and more *for the specific job* they’re seeking to fill.”  
   2. Strong keywords  
   3. Less is more: this is what I want to be found for.  
2. **Demonstrate engagement** with: the firm, “network node” connections, job market generally  
   1. A seemingly disengaged candidate fails to send the right signals on these three dimensions of the employer, the people, and the market.  
3. Find and **cultivate warm lead** at every target employer  
   1. Employee referrals are the best sources of talent with the highest return on investment. Referrals are validation-providing and confidence-boosting. They offer comfort to hirers.  
   2. Find organizational insiders to refer or vouch for you  
   3. Level doesn’t matter as much as speed. Most first-round decisions about who gets in for the firs twitch of interviews are made within the first 72 to 96 hours that a job posting is live  
   4. Ideally one solid relationship to refer to internal process *before* role is posted \- but if you haven’t “It’s more important that you get your application in within the first four days and work simultaneously to have a current employee flag your materials and candidacy in the meantime--or worst case, in week two.”  
4. Build both **your network** and **your understanding** of the company, industry, and people  
   1. Being 1st-degree-connected to recruiters and hiring managers--or at least those who *they* are connected to--ranks you higher in the search results.” Like Google, be in first 2 pages of results  
   2. **Network effect** \- the phenomenon of everyone benefiting from something the more it’s used.   
      1. Broader, more diverse networks are stronger. Connect broadly; very liberally  
      2. Network intentionally, not just randomly \-= purposeful network with the right people  
      3. “By networking with them,you'll be able to build your knowledge about their companies, their industries, and the movers and shakers that drive them.”  
   3. **Weak ties** \- the 2nd and 3rd degree connections that give us the volume we need to peer into millions of people’s network for precious referrals  
5. Do your **own background check** \- most crucially during the offer stage  
   1. Be proactive and on the hunt for data that can help you get the job & decide whether to take it.  
   2. Contacting *former* employees of company  
   3. Insights from shared connections to the manager or other team members on LinkedIn  
   4. Knowing your worth for salary negotiations  
   5. Additional insider information from review sites like Glassdoor (fishbowl, reddit)  
   6. When managers start to home in on a candidate, they shift from being the consumer … to the seller (trying to convince candidates that this is a great role, team , and organization.  
   7. Background check is about arming yourself with the knowledge to make a decision \- and to avoid an unpleasant situation before it potentially happens.

#### **Glassdoor: Half-Truths, Half-Useful**

1. Outlier Bias  
   1. Only people who have had a great or awful experience are most motivated to post.  
   2. Negativity bias skews towards awful experiences  
2. Generic vs. specific  
   1. No organization is a monolith  
   2. “Any organization is really just a bunch of individual teams. It’s those teams, and their specific managers and the microcultures within them, that will affect your day-to-day work and your happiness more than any generic review.”

## The Mind of a Recruiter

### Insight \#1: Recruiters *must* say No

Charged with whittling down from average \~250 applications to 1 true hire for 30-40 jobs at once.

“Recruiters need to reject more than 99 percent of applicants by the end of the hiring process, so their antenna is always up for reasons to say “No” to a candidate. And because they’re often trying to fill lots of open job requisitions at once, they also need to reject candidates as fast as possible. 

Never give them a reason to ding you outright \[on\]:

- Spelling errors  
- Wrong company on cover letter  
- No headshot photo (or not at good one)  
- No relevant experience in background  
- Not explaining exactly what you did in previous roles  
- Not demonstrating clear impact in previous roles.

### Insight \#2: Recruiters are risk-averse.

- You fill the recruiting funnel with presumable no-brainers. 

**The Obvious Candidate.** “Nobody ever got fired for choosing IBM”.

- “Someone who has connected the dots in a purposeful way for the job the recruiter is trying to fill, versus throwing spaghetti at the wall and hoping something sticks. Someone who, usually, has already *done* the job being filled and has the requisite skills.” p. 92  
  - Understand precisely what the recruiter is searching for  
  - Signal that you match those attributes in the exact places they’re looking.

#### **Tips from a Longtime Recruiter**

1. First in, first hired.  
   1. Recruiters are incentivized to hire quickly \- do not delay.   
2. Show me the (realistic) money.   
   1. Know the salary range for the role (glassdoor, linkedin, [salary.com](http://salary.com). levels.fyi)  
   2. Don’t put a number down if not required (say you can negotiate later)  
   3. Don’t be first to mention salary  
3. Ready candidate is hired candidate  
   1. Will screen out anything beyond reasonable 2-4 weeks. Respond “Available Immediately”  
   2. Not doing so makes  you sound unserious

### Insight \#3: Recruiters need tech tools

LinkedIn enables recruiters to filter down to 5-10 best-fit candidates instead of manually sifting through 250+ random applications.

- Instant filtering is the primary value proposition for LinkedIn to make its money \- why every recruiter pays LinkedIn $10K per year to use.  
- With a single search on this tech tool … recruiters can narrow down their search to just the best-fit candidates

#### **What about the Nonprofit Job search**

## Referrals: The Biggest Advantage

Savvy job searchers know that who gets picked isn’t a meritocracy. They also know that the shortlist of people under consideration consists of those who are already in the inner circle--because search results are ordered by connection proximity *to the person doing the search*.

The most important of these human inputs to hirers is the referral. Getting someone on the inside to both vouch and advocate for you turns out to be the single best way to get an edge in the whole job search process.

Jobvite data: Applications → Hires:

- Referrals: 7% → 40%  
- Career sites: 32% → 21%  
- Job boards: 43% → 15%  
- Agencies: 2% → 5%  
- Other: 16% → 19%.

75% of applications come from online portals, but only 36% of offers  
Actual relationships have outsize results

Fore hirers, relationships de-risk candidates. … it’s that hirers almost always have to choose between several solid candidates, so  relationships become the differentiator. And a referral gets you not just a serious look but a look in the first place. … 

- Depth of connection to the employee, or others at the firm, isn’t really a criterion; the mere act of a referral is all  the validation they need.

From the perspectives of each stakeholder:

- Company \- Referred candidates cost less to recruit, perform better, are hired faster and stay on the job longer. **86%** of recruiters said referrals were \#1 source of high quality candidates  
- Referrer \- typically generates a bonus of 1-5k  
- Job seeker: a referral takes you from a random outsider to a preferred insider; skyrocketing chances

Employee referrals are treated almost the same as internal transfers: as easy-to-validate and “safe” candidates. You do yourself a massive disservice by not doing everything you can to secure a referral for a role you're pumped about.

#### **Timing your referral right**

This timing lag can become a serious issue, so negotiating any tricky timing:

- If you already know someone at the company, have them refer you right away.

## Crushing the Interview

1. ### Prepare for the **tell me about yourself** question

- Starting point \- interviewers assessing your overall narrative and whether you can be crisp and compelling.   
- The onus is on you to paint a beautiful picture of best skills & experiences.  
- Connect the dots right from the get-go

2. ### **First impressions** matter

- A third of interviewers know in the first 90 seconds whether or not they’re going to hire a candidate.  
- Demonstrate competence by being purposeful  
- Narrative should be directed \- use highly relevant examples  
- Exude warmth to make a human connection … think about the stories and pieces of your work or academic history that are most compelling to you.

3. ### Avoid overly generic responses

- A common pitfall: failing to litter your answers with concrete examples that show, not just tell, what you’re capable of.  
- Think through how to make these examples malleable so you can insert them into the conversation  
- “Problem-Action-Result” format \- vague response →\< specific  
- (1) success/achievement you’re particularly proud of, (2) roadblock you overcame or tough decision you made  
- Describe your experiences so you focus less on your past job responsibilities, and more on your accomplishments. 

4. ### Be deliberately **self-promotional**

- A savvy interviewee knows it’s also an opportunity to drive home the right points in order to stand out and maximally strengthen their candidacy  
- An interview is not the time to be too humble

5. ### Look for common interview **questions online**

   1. Glassdoor, LinkedIn  
   2. Irreplaceable value of an insider

6. ### **Questions you ask** can be as important as the questions you answer

   1. Best interviews feel more like conversation than quiz show: Sense the vibe.  
   2. This section of the interview is crucial to the overall impression you make. Like the ‘tmay’ question, don’t approach it lazily. Have 10 solid questions in your back pocket, ranked by priority, in case some get addressed during the course of the interview.  
   3. Having made sure your questions are tailored to each interviewer by looking into their backgrounds; show your ability to think critically about the company, the team, the challenges they're facing.  
   4. Three bold questions:  
      1. Do I have the job?  
      2. Is there anything we discussed today that makes you feel that I am not the one for the job?

7. ### Don’t forget **the (speedy) follow-up.**

   1. Thank the interviewer in a short but substantive and authentic way  
   2. The days of a personalized card arriving four days later are long gone, and speed of follow-up is key. Hiring decisions can be made almost immediately.
