# Introduction

The job search has gotten *way* more complex in the last thirty years… the same amount of time that used to *just* go toward:

- Resumes  
- Cover Letters  
- Interview Prep

…must now include:

- LinkedIn  
- Online Job Search Engines  
- Resume Keyword Optimization  
- Social Media, etc…

…each of those items has a different *level* of importance…. Modern job seekers must figure out how to allocate their limited time and energy across all the various job search skills according to each one’s relative importance.

These frameworks are indeed more like baking (where precision is critical) than cooking (where approximation is OK). 

***IDEO’s Project Mood Chart***

- Y-axis \- mood of teams  
- X-axis \- duration of a challenging project  
- Follows a basic “U”-shaped curve \- peaks at both ends and a valley in the middle.  
- First peak \- Hope  
- Valley of Insight \- negative, problems. “Learning isn’t always fun, but it is the precursor to proficiency”  
- Second peak \- Confidence

…spending a dozen or so hours on self-analysis to identify your preferred work environments, locations, or responsibilities might have been a justifiable exercise a few years ago. However, today we are in the age of **monopsony** (think “buyer’s market”), in which larger employers can control the type, number, and pay of jobs they make available, These larger employers have done all they can to replace high-paying jobs with low-paying ones and eliminate others to save on costs. … 

“I’d rather work forward from the worst-case scenario (undesired unemployment) as a starting point to try to find a career that captures as many bright spots as possible in the shortest amount of time.

- We can focus on what we’ll enjoy from next job  
- Then refine goals as we go along  
- Factoring in any complications/insights along the way

***In the end, the best way to identify and evaluate potential careers is to talk to people who currently have them to see if what they like about their work resonates with you.***

- Ultimately, employers don’t hire people, *people* hire people. Thus, you need to get to know them and vice versa. \[p. 9\]

Networking \- Like flu shots  
***For every job that goes to an online applicant, twelve jobs go to internal referrals. Networking is the only way to systematically get referrals, so transitively it is the only systematic way to get interviews.***

Before we start developing referrals: narrow scope to 3 candidate careers.  
**LAMP List (List-Alumni-Motivation-Posting)** \- 70 minutes of effort

- Swapping perfection for speed, identifying forty target employers and three pieces of data for each that are easy to find and predictive of success

By focusing on no more than 3 career paths spread across 5 different target employers), you ensure you don’t spread yourself too thin, ensuring you  learn as you go.

## Technique \#1: You Bet Your Life Exercise {#technique-#1:-you-bet-your-life-exercise}

1. Open stopwatch: 1 minute.  
2. Name a single professional skill/ability in which you are most confident you’re in the top 1%of the world.  
- Time pressure \+ focus on one superlative skill \> laundry list \= clarity  
- Make sure that any job you accept in the future takes advantage of this one skill.  
- Career selection approach is:  
- Iterative \- we’ll investigate a few different options partway before committing to any one  
- Bottom-up \- we’ll start from nothing and identify the cornerstones of our future career (rather than starting from ideal job and making concessions”

**Key Benefit:** Cutting through the noise. If your ability to provide for yourself and your loved ones depends on one thing, this should be it.

## Technique \#2: Brain Dump Exercise {#technique-#2:-brain-dump-exercise}

1. Starting right now, write down every job that you think you would enjoy doing and could feasibly be or become qualified for one day.  
   1. Keep going until you literally can’t think of any more  
   2. Every single thing  
   3. ***Brain dump*** \- dump everything out of your head and onto a sheet of paper or spreadsheet.  
2. Then, set the list aside; feel free to add to it over the next day or two  
   1. This act alone helps you get outside your own head  
   2. Running out of new ideas makes it easier to focus on one or more of your favorites from the list 

**Key Benefit:** Getting out of your own head. Turning a seemingly infinite number of options into a finite list of possibilities.

## Technique \#2: StrengthsFinder \+ “How Your Talents Add Value”

*Positive psychology* \- we have more growth potential in enhancing our natural abilities than we do in shoring up our weaknesses.

### StrengthsFinder {#strengthsfinder}

[**StrengthsFinder Quiz**](https://www.gallup.com/cliftonstrengths/en/254033/strengthsfinder.aspx) \- 177 question [Gallup](https://high5test.com/cliftonstrengths-free/) quiz: 5 out of 34 talent themes you most strongly possess

- **Strengths** \= Innate talents \+ focused effort  
- 1 / 275k have the same Top 5; 1 / 33.4 million have the same prioritized order.  
- Top 5 talent themes interact with one another, rather than presenting themselves the same way in anyone who possesses them  
- Purchasing fully ordered list: detailed top 10 report \+ *least* developed themes (make/buy decision)

### How your Talents add Value {#how-your-talents-add-value}

1. Pick *2* of top 5 talent themes you must regularly combine in order to accomplish each bucket of work.   
   1. **4 buckets of work:**  
      Making things happen  
      Collaborating with others  
      Leading & Influencing others  
      Solving challenging problems  
2. Put in your own words how you used these talent themes in tandem to do good work  
3. Describe an example where you did so.  
- *How* we go about accomplishing \[4 buckets of work\] is unique. Personal style refined over decades.  
- Gives you confidence to answer “yes” when asked if you know how your talents add value to orgs.  
- Gives you ownable language that is more insightful than job seeker cliches.   
- Knowing your signature style makes \[other job search prep\] easier

\[The 2 exercises\] will give you suggestions for work situations in which you will naturally and intuitively thrive, making it easier for you to evaluate potential career paths for both sustainability and personal fulfillment.

***It’s less about what your Top 5 are and more about whether you know and can explain how you’ll adapt them to the working circumstances of a particular role.*** 

## Technique \#4: Informational meetings {#technique-#4:-informational-meetings}

***Informational meetings*** \- a form of information gathering in which you control the agenda

- Informationals allow job seekers to learn about potential careers/employers by asking employees:  
   why they enjoy what they do where they do it,  
- ideally in a systematic and purposeful fashion

**TIARA (Trends-Insights-Advice-Resources-Assignments)** 

- Provides a road map for turning strangers into advocates within a single 30 minute informational  
- Experts in their fields → Potential mentors  
- Subtle script for “Why are you so good at your job”

Informationals serve double duty

- Makes you smarter about the space you’re seeking to enter  
- Gives you one more set of eyes and ears watching out for job opportunities on your behalf  
- Particularly helpful if list of top-tier potential career paths \> 3

## Technique \#5: Reading Mindfully {#technique-#5:-reading-mindfully}

1. Do the normal business/industry-related reading in your spare time (books, magazines, internet, etc.)  
2. Start habit of ***reading mindfully*** \- whenever you read about an organization doing interesting work, make a note of that org’s name and sector, as potential employer  
3. Once a habit is established, you’ll spot some patterns \- will help clarify genuine interests.

Each technique provides a meaningful data point, but considered together they start to tell a story that helps you expand your consideration set and escape the box your environment may have unwittingly or unwittingly created for you.

# Chapter 2: Resumes

# Chapter 2: Resumes

…there’s no such thing as a perfect resume. Even if it were possible, it wouldn’t be worth the effort.  
***On average hiring managers spent six seconds per resume***.

- **80% of attention focused on:**  
  - Candidate name  
  - Employer name  
  - Job Title(s)  
  - Dates of employment  
  - Schools attended  
- **4.8s / 6s \= unchangeable; 1.2s / 6s \= bullet points**

### easy-to-defend candidates

- Hiring managers are looking for **easy-to-defend candidates**, not perfectly worded bullets \- at least in their initial passes through a stack of resumes.  
- Could never embarrass a hiring manager, no matter how poorly their interview, due to their pedigree or previous experience.  
- Hierarchy:  
  - Candidates who have done the job before somewhere else  
  - Worked for elite employers  
  - Attended elite schools  
- No hiring manager wants to suffer the embarrassment of bringing in a novelty candidate who doesn’t match the easy-to-defend descriptions, only to have them perform terribly in the interview.

### Goldilocks zone

Resumes are necessary evil: hit the **Goldilocks zone** as quickly and efficiently as possible. Neither:

- Underprepared \- causing unprofessional first impressions; nor  
- Overprepared \- at cost of higher value activities

Done \= *objectively* perfect, *subjectively* good enough.

- Objective perfection: proper spelling, grammar, margin alignment, internally consistent formatting, single font.

**Basic Resume \-** 100% error-free resume \= Minimum Viable Product 

- Baseline \- worst-case scenario  
- The only time employers ever mentioned resumes … was when they contained errors

**Good Resume**  \- 80/20

- Designed to work well enough for most job seekers  
- Requiring as little time and effort as possible to prepare

| Content | Basic Resume \- MVP | Good Resume \- 80/20 | Great Resume \- little marginal impact |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Formatting** | Error-Free | Error-Free | Error-Free |
| Bullet **Source** | Job Description | Annual Review | “Greatest hits” |
| Bullets **Describe…** | Responsibilities | Major projects | Impact (+ root causes) |
| **Results** are… | Not addressed | Provided when quantitative | Always provided |

**Three-Hour Rule** \- Resume should take no more than 3 out of 100 hours of job search

- Online postings have *commoditized* resumes … a nice resume isn’t enough … to score an interview, you need a perfect background or referral.

***Cobra effect / perverse incentives** \-* the perverse incentives CVs provide are the illusion of control and a reason to delay. … The resume is the one part of your job search process you control.

- The thought of networking … requires admitting the process is outside control … terrifies, so want to ensure that the CV is perfect before being put in a position of rejection.”

Resumes should be:

- Easy to read  
- Broken up with headers  
- Feature some white space  
- Resume (chronological) template in MS word as starting point

## **Objectives**

- **Delete\!**  
- They typically tend to become repositories for buzzwords and cliches, featuring subjective terms … that are rather vapid and impossible to substantiate.  
- If kept, **keep it objective, down to one line**  
- **Use to mention title of targeted role**  
- If \> 10 mins, finish rest of resume first; re-evaluate

When hiring managers use an ATS (applicant Tracking System)… they are looking for ***Objective*** **criteria**:

- **Job title** in past experience  
  - **Specific degree** or credential  
  - People who **worked for competitor**  
  - (not subjective info)

## **Skills & Abilities**

- **Delete\!**  
- Anything of value here will show up elsewhere in resume  
- Will need the space for actual accomplishments 

## **Experience**

- Prevented in reverse chronological order  
- One-page resume is fine for most everyone outside academia  
- \<35 y.o.: down to 1 page  
- Cut the less impressive versions of similar projects  
- **“Greatest hits” \> professional hours log**

### Accomplishments \> Responsibilities

***Accomplishment statement \-*** a description of the impact you made with the responsibilities you had

- Differentiates Good and Great resumes from basic  
- Responsibility statements \- passive phrasing (responsible for), weak verbs (coordinated, oversaw)  
  - Describes responsibilities and theoretical duties rather than actual actions taken  
- Get specific:   
  - instead of talking about what you were *supposed to do* generally,   
  - discuss what you ***actually did specifically***\--the ***projects*** you’d list on an annual review, and  
  - ***key actions*** taken to complete them.  
- Good → Great: Clarifying ***why*** each bullet point is impressive  
  - Specific actions \+ impact achieved  
  - Fewer bullet points, higher quality  
  - Ensure only best accomplishments are “at risk” of being asked about in an interview  
  - **Highlight one time you executed that activity the best:** Specificity \= memorable \+ credible  
    - the particular iteration of a project you did most effectively, and   
    - what specific insights you had that enabled exceptional results \-   
  - Great resumes don’t stop w/ responsibilities (basic) or projects (good), they finish w/ results:  
  - ***What exceptional results did you*** **achieve *with those responsibilities and opportunities?***

### Results

- Reduce risk  
- Improve efficiency  
- Simplify tasks  
- Increase knowledge

Approximate without exact numbers \- just make sure it’s error-free.

- The more time you spend wordsmithing, the longer your resume gets, the higher the error probability

Tools to capture objective errors (\>improving subjective content)

- MS Word spell-check  
- [Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/)  
- [VMock](https://www.vmock.com/)

## **Education**

- **Keep:** School \+ Major  
- 1-2 lines max.: GPA, extracurriculars, awards, exchange programs

## **Additional Information**

- **Must-Have**  
- ….the one part of a resume that makes you seem like a real person instead of a theoretical concept  
- Info included here \= subject of small talk at start of interview \- professional informs, builds rapport  
  - Professionally informs \- any other salient information that informs the employer about your ability to do the work for which you are putting yourself forward  
- Put each  different topic on a separate line w/ bolded title (e.g., **Programming Languages:,** etc.)  
- \> 5  lines of content   
- **Hobbies**: 1-2 lines that focus on personally meaningful, rapport-building topics (within 5 line limit)

### Other headers

- Entrepreneurial Experience / Community Experience  
- Describe work experience in years only (no months) \- make employment gaps disappear, easier to read

## **CAR (Challenge-Action-Result) Matrix:**

- Each bullet point represents a story  
- CAR \- industry standard format for behavioral interviews   
- (Tell me about a time / Give me an example of when you…)  
1. Start creating a spreadsheet with each story in its own row  
2. Start brainstorming now  
- Start with the stories that come to mind if having trouble coming up with bullets

**Write accomplishment statements for your bullets *now--*either in a CAR Matrix or directly into your resume--so you don’t have to do it later.**

## **Troubleshooting**

### Identifying accomplishments:

1. Think smaller: every project: research, strategy, implementation. Discuss each phase as a story.  
2. Review behavioral interview questions \- will spark memories of professional experiences  
3. Take Strengthsfinder quiz, review results \- highlight skills as natural venues for accomplishments.

### Too much time on resume:

- Aim for Good Resume status:  
- Look at past annual reviews  
- Summarize cleverest actions to complete those projects  
- Open old job descriptions and pull language  
- Upload resume draft into a resume analysis tool to see / incorporate suggestions

# Chapter 3: LinkedIn Profile

# Chapter 3: LinkedIn Profile

Overall, we’ll adopt a mind-set very similar to what we have for our resume--objectively perfect, subjectively good enough. 

Remember, what employers are typically looking for on LinkedIn is your *objective* experience: job titles held, employers worked for, schools attended, and certifications and degrees attained. 

Professional headshot  
…wear the clothes you would  expect to wear if hired for your desired role, and get a variety of shots from the elbows up as well as more tightly framed on your face so you have some options.

- You want to appear approachable, so don’t be formal--relax and smile. 

The easiest version of a good-enough headline … is an *objective* description of the experience you’ve had to date. The simplest version of this is just your current job title and employer. 

Give yourself options … Unfortunately, aspirational headlines like “Seeking new opportunity in product management” don’t effectively convey confidence. 

- My favorite approach here is a broad description of you as a professional in fifteen words or less. It could be one phrase or a collection of descriptors.

Write down a llt of phrases that you *might* use just to see which ones resonate with you, rather than trying to come up with a good headline on your first try.

- Take the next minute and just start writing or typing phrases that are ownable and unique to you.  
- It’s really just something that  
- (1) does *not advertise* you are looking for a job and   
- (2) gives people a sense of who you are and what you are about.

Include authentic phrases that are true to you rather than phrases you think a potential employer wants to hear

## About section

Keep it pretty short \- A line or two summarizing your background and experience will suffice.  
On the other hand, if you have more to write and using this section feels authentic to you, give it a shot, but note that the risk here is that it gets so long that nobody wants to read it.

Just accept that your About section will never be perfect; it will just eat up as much time as you give to it. … In this book, we are focused strictly on getting to networking as quickly as possible without doing ourselves any material harm.

## Experience section

It’s an error-free way to communicate your objective career information; anything beyond that is gravy. 

The lovely thing about LinkedIn is that it is an *abridged* resume. It gives you the information you want to know in a predictable format, and if you want to read (or write) more, the option is there.

So, from an efficiency perspective, three options:

1. Include *only* objective information (employers, job titles, locations, and employment dates).  
2. Include *select* bullets from your resume (the one you put at the top is usually your best one, so feel free to go with that).  
3. Fully replicate your resume in your LinkedIn profile (but use the same language, to avoid double  work).

Pick whichever approach feels most natural to you, but again, don’t fall into the quagmire of trying to create new and original content for LinkedIn. Your (error-free) objective content is what’s important.

## Education section

- Facts of your education are what count: schools attended, degrees earned, areas of particular study.  
- Grad year is totally optional.  
- Mention info about your formative educational extracurriculars here

## Licenses & certifications

Include if relevant.

## Volunteer Experience section

- If you have a community / volunteer experience section on yorur resume, this will bolster your years of experience and give people a sense of the causes tht are important to you and demonstrate that you have taken leadership roles in addressing them.

## Skills & Endorsements, Recommendations

- Drives traffic on LinkedIn

## Accomplishments, Interests, etc.

- Look through the options for low-hanging fruit \- awards, publications, foreign language abilities  
- Simply list the objective information that’s easy to include, and save the subjective information or items requiring explanation after your networking efforts are already under way.

# Chapter 4: Cover Letters

# Chapter 4: Cover Letters

They illustrate how well you can construct an argument (when responding to the question “Why should we consider *you*?” specifically). The cover letter informs the employer about your knowledge of the role and how your skills make you well suited for it. Furthermore, it offers insight into your communication skills.  
Informs employer:

- How well you can construct an argument  
- Your knowledge of the role  
- How your skills make you well-suited for it  
- Offers insight into communication skills: a free writing sample

## Reason-Anecdote-Connection (RAC) Model

Cover letter itself is answering the “Why should we consider *you*?” question \- consider it your prompt.

Unfacilitated \- submit with a resume to an online job posting rather than to a person

- “You should not be writing many unfacilitated cover letters” … relying instead on the advocates you develop to make the case for you.

Facilitated \- ask to attach in an email with resume so they can simply forward it for you

## RAC Letters (unfacilitated)

- 200 to 300 words

Five paragraph cover letter:

- 1: Introductory paragraph  
- 2-4: Three RAC paragraphs  
  - systematically detail why you think you’d be a good fit  
- 5: Short closing paragraph  
  - Asking for an interview and providing contact info

### Introductory Paragraph

Should be “lean and mean”

- **State the position** you’re interested in  
- Any **key advocates** at the organization  
- An expression of belief that **you can add value** “in the following ways”  
- Optional: include any single story, reason, or  major motivation:  
- Why *this* position, Why *now*

### RAC paragraphs

1. **Reason (1st sentence):** State a skill or attribute that you think would make you appealing to the employer  
   1. Bolded word or short phrase followed by colon (e.g., **Leadership:**, “**Comfort with ambiguity:**”)  
2. **Anecdote (2nd sentence)**: Why you think employer should find your Reason *true* or *important*. Forms:  
   1. Resume bullet point summary \- more natural language explaining skill/attribute  
   2. Accolade/award: demonstrating proficiency at that award  
   3. Information you learned \- from online research or informational meeting \- reinforcing that Reason within their organization  
   4. Anecdotes are proof:  
      1. Proof that you possess that skill or attribute  
      2. Proof that your Reason matters to the employer  
3. **Connection:** optional, if word count allows \- connects your Reason to something the employer cares about (if not immediately obvious), e.g., increased sales, decreased errors, better team, etc. Demonstrate:  
   1. You understand the job you’re applying for  
   2. Appreciate that the employer is the ultimate audience of the letter

### Closing paragraph

- Think it would be a great fit  
- Contact information

### ***Connecting resume bullets to cover letters***

Think of your resume bullets as a list of ingredients and your cover letter as a free sample of the dish you’d prepare *from* those ingredients. Use the cover letter to add color and flavor by better establishing the degree of difficulty of a particular accomplishment or by positioning yourself as *uniquely* qualified, knowledgeable, or motivated in a specific area, rather than just proficient.

Commentary (such as clarifying youngest) that might be too subjective for a resume … such context is totally appropriate and adds helpful texture to cover letter RACs for clarifying why your reason is valid.

### Choosing 3 Reasons:

Spend time clarifying 

- Why that Reason is specific to *them* as a candidate (Anecdote)  
  - Why a particular accomplishment was especially challenging or impressive  
- Why the employer should care (Connection)  
  - Connect them back to the employer’s needs

Points of Parity \+ Points of Differentiation

- Points of parity \- elements shared by everyone   
- Points of Differentiation \- make a product unique   
  - 

**Point of differentiation** approach: pick 3 Reasons in which you are at the very top of the candidate pool.

- Then, devote your time & effort toward explaining how the employer will benefit from having that exceptional attribute of yours inside their organization.  
- Pick reasons: that genuinely reflect your biggest strengths \> what you think the employer wants to hear

RAC is definitely formulaic, but not impersonal.. … it allows you to focus on personalization rather than word count or format”

- This helps you convey your rationale directly and authentically, minimizing the need to include buzzwords and jargon.

### Example

Dear Franklin,

I am interested in Darlington’s open Marketing Associate opposition. After speaking with current associates Angela Charles and Nicole Yazzie, I believe I can contribute the following to your organization.

**Problem Solving:** I received two promotions in three years--the fastest rate possible at Biffco--for my ability to break complex equipment issues into solvable tasks. My ability in this area would help me keep complex projects such as product redesigns and packaging changes running smoothly and on time at Darlington.

**Analytics**: While at BiffCo, I reduced part costs 30 percent by analyzing more than 12,000 unique inventory items to phase out obsolete parts, identify cheaper substitutes, and renegotiate contracts on our most heavily purchased items. The ability to use large amounts of data in creative ways would help me identify new markets and customer segments for Darlington’s products.

**Leadership:** Nicole stressed to me how much Darlington values leadership in the form of firm-building. I have always strived to leave my mark on my organizations, both as Technology Officer in my graduate school’s student government and as Excel Training Leader for BiffCos’s new analysts. At Darlington, I would continue seeking leadership opportunities to strengthen the firm and build my internal network.

In closing, I think this would be a great mutual fit. Please contact me at 555-555-55555 or [email@email.com](mailto:email@email.com) at your earliest convenience.

Best regards,  
Aditya

Use your cover letter to tell readers what your resume *means to them*.

- Interpret it for them (as you would your findings from a project in an email to your boss’s boss)  
- Tell them what conclusions you suggest they draw based on the data provided (( \> introducing data w/o analysis/perspective)

## Facilitated cover letter

A facilitated cover letter can consist of a brief self-contained email (with resume attached( that your advocate can forward to HR with minimal additional commentary (read: effort)). Thus it behooves you to keep it short and inoffensive. … allow their advocacy to do most of the talking.

1. Acknowledge your advocate’s request for your resume  
2. Reiterate your interest in the specific role you would like to be considered for.  
3. Ask if they need additional information to keep the process moving forward.

### Example

“Mariela,  
Thank you for your insights during our conversation earlier this week--it was both informative and motivating. On further reflection, I would indeed like to be considered for the open Marketing Associate position at Darlington.  
Per your request, I am attaching my resume. Do you need anything else from me at this time? Please let me know if you have additional questions, and I look forward to discussing our next steps.

Best regards,  
Aditya”

“As with resumes \- it is critical your cover letters are error-free. ..  
 If your advocate spots a typo in your cover letter … Your gaffe would damage their peers’ *perception of them*, so you might lose your advocate’s willingness to fight for you”

## Troubleshooting

Advocate says to apply online rather than through them?

1. Not truly an advocate…but still mention them in your cover letter.  
2. It may be that you must apply online before they can advocate for you.

## **Small Talk** {#small-talk}

- First impressions, introductions, seats taken, initial rapport  
- They lead, you follow  
- **Temperature-check questions**

***Confirmation Bias*** \- humanity’s tendency to arrive at a quick first impression about various people and subjects and then only pay attention to evidence that reinforces our first impressions while ignoring information that contradicts these impressions.

- Key is to make sure it’s working for rather than against us.

**Temperature Check** “how is your day going so far?

- Help preview conversational style / helpfulness  
- Give a bit of personal information for partner to react  
- Seize bits of extraneous information to build rapport through follow-up questions  
- “Yes, and” your way into a meaningful interaction  
- Listening \> speaking; build on comments interviewer makes

## **Tell Me about Yourself (TMAY)** {#tell-me-about-yourself-(tmay)}

- Single most likely interview question to be asked \- 60% of interview decisions  
- Answer given some thought in advance.  
- Completely unique question \- use it for rapport-building, transition in to sales

Bad ways to answer 

- The Improv \- On-the-spot improv lacks structure, clarity, impact; Spontaneity is too casual  
- The Sales Pitch \- guard goes from sales-pitch; success means bringing interviewer’s guard down.   
- The Transcript \- dry, mental conversion of TMAY into WMTYR, reciting facts already in resume  
  - Focuses on *what* job seeker did, as opposed to *why* they did it.

Interviewers just want to know that your overall story makes sense. … They genuinely want to know:

- Who you are at the core:  
- What motivates you and your decisions  
- Why this organization and role make sense at this point in your life  
- Tell them about *yourself* \> companies, job titles, responsibilities.

At times, \[interviewers\] will even ***prefer*** **frameworks if they help to convey critical information in an easy-to-understand fashion**. … if you don’t use \[given frameworks\], interviewers will actively dislike your answers, since they have to work harder to understand the information you’re trying to convey.

- Authenticity is a matter of how you use it.  
- Frameworks deliver relevant information concisely and acclimate interviewer to a rhythm

**Responses: 2 minutes per question.**

Creating storyline response

- Elements of story (beats) Connected by **“But,” “Therefore” \> “and then”**  
- One element leads logically into the next \= satisfying memorable story  
- Characters acted logically based on what we’ve learned  
- Information delivered in narrative fashion (woven into a story) was 6-7x more memorable than a list

### **FIT** Model \= **Favorite** part \- **Insight** gained \- **Transition** Made

- Natural and authentic but structured enough framework to deliver information in likable way  
- Will help to maintain rapport, quickly catch up on how you ended up here with them in this moment  
1. **Brief orienting sentence** \- wherever “hero’s journey” began \- hometown, college, etc.  
   - Feels authentic \+ allows response in 2 mins  
   - Concise and *doesn’t* sell  
   - Purpose: impart key background on you rather than “establish themes” or impress interviewer  
   - Keep it short: Story \> detailed facts  
2. **Favorite** \- part of that job or life stage  
   - Favorite \> “passionate about” or “really enjoy”  
   - Only 1 favorite: genuineness removes temptation to laundry list  
   - Ideal humblebrag: employers assume you’re pretty good at it  
   - One less thing to remember in interview \- reduces memorization: authenticity \> specific claim  
3. **Insight (“but”)** \- information gained / lesson learned that led to change in status quo:  
   - Never skip: You made decisions every step of the way \- explains why.  
   - Promotions \- one of the only critical things to mention during answer \- celebrate/highlight  
     - Demonstrate that your org liked you enough to pay more / give more responsibility  
     - “I was seeking more responsibility” / “manage a larger team” / “gain exposure”  
     - → Transition: “\[so happily accepted promotion\]”  
   - Layoffs: Insight gained → Improvement Desired  
     - Avoid mentioning if authentic; if not, address directly as Insight/Improvement in FIT  
   - Authenticity is key … even if you didn’t choose \[to be downsized\], **you chose how to respond** to downsizing, and that in turn informs your interviewer about the kind of candidate to whom they are speaking.  
   - Reframe **negative → positive**: Find something positive to say about every experience, frame insights around things we wanted to do differently or more consistently rather than around what we disliked or were running away from.  
4. **Transition (“therefore”)** \- into your next stage  
- Start with a logical transitional word like “therefore,” “thus,” \- make it easy for listener to know that Transition resulted directly from Insight  
5. **Finish**: by leading into “Why this role?” question  
- Makes transition from TMAY into Why this role feel like spontaneous convo  
- *Tease* interest in role, don’t speak at length \- allow interviewer to ask, then take full 2 minutes

## **Why Questions** {#why-questions}

### **RAC** Model \= **Reason**\-**Anecdote**(-**Connection**) \= **Authentic** \+ **Specific** \+ **Informed**

- Limit to Reason \+ Anecdote \- connection not always necessary/possible given 2 min time limit  
1. **Reasons \-** Start with \# of reasons (**3**) \- omne trium perfectum  
   1. Helps break up answers into more memorable 30-40s chunks \= 2 mins.  
   2. Brain dump \- start by making a list of every Reason you can think of why you want this job, organization, and this sector or industry  
2. **Anecdotes \-** Responses to “Why?” Must be:  
   1. Authentic   
      1. who you are, what motivates you, why this makes sense  
   2. Specific (to role, employer, sector, industry)  
      1. Attribute answers / Anecdotes to specific instances (e.g., informational meeting)  
   3. Informed   
      1. employer/industry research should be done w/ express purpose of answering why questions in authentic, specific, informed way  
3. **Connection** (when to use/add)  
   1. Most effective when answer might be trite / cliched  
   2. When the benefit of your Reason to the employer is not immediately clear
