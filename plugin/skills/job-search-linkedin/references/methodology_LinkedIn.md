## 1. The core model — what a LinkedIn profile is actually for

Every source converges on the same claim, phrased differently. A LinkedIn profile has one job:
**get you into the small set of candidates a recruiter contacts.**

The corollaries, in order of how much they should change your behavior:

**1. Discoverability is a gate, not a gradient.** A profile that never surfaces in a recruiter's
search scores zero on every downstream quality, however well written. This is why "Job Title,
Location, Skills" (Schifeling: *"there are really only three things they search for that you can
control"*) carries more weight than everything else combined.

**2. Recruiters are looking for reasons to say No.** Dalton's framing. ~250 applicants per corporate
opening, 4–6 interviewed, 1 offer (*Linked*). The average recruiter juggles ~40 open roles and spends
~6 seconds on initial review (*Career Coach GPT*). **Defects are disqualifying in a way that
brilliance is not compensating.** A typo is a free reason to say No.

**3. The recruiter will not connect your dots.** *Linked*: *"recruiters and hiring managers are
perpetually overwhelmed, so they need an easily discernible narrative onto which they can latch. A
surefire way to* not *stand out is to expect the recruiter to connect the dots of your career."*
This is the single most-repeated idea across all nine books.

**4. Perceived specialists get traction.** *Linked*: *"Less is more — cut through noise only by being
found / known for a specific thing. Perceived specialists are the ones who get traction in the job
market. Perception is reality."* Directly opposes the instinct to list everything you can do.

**5. Inbound is not the main channel — but it makes the main channel easier.** ≥70% of hires happen
where the candidate has a personal connection; referred candidates carry roughly a 10× advantage
(*Linked*). Mann: *"Applying online is the slow way. Networking is the fast way. Measure your
progress in interactions, not applications."* **A 90/100 profile is not a substitute for talking to
people.** It is what makes those conversations start warm rather than cold.

## 2. The Board of Advisors — what each source uniquely contributes

Ordered by how load-bearing they are for LinkedIn specifically.

### Garriott & Schifeling — *Linked* ⭐ backbone
The most LinkedIn-specific source. Contributes: the recruiter's mental model ("Resume-PLUS" — resume
plus human narrative plus aspiration), the two hiring questions (*Could this person do the job?* /
*Am I excited about this person?*), the personal-brand components (story + niche + custom URL +
keywords + completeness + show-don't-tell), and the "Old Game vs. New Game" table whose most relevant
row is: **custom cover letter → "write a thoughtful, keyword-laden About section."**

Also contributes the *equity* caveat, which no other source raises: the algorithm favors people
already proximate to influential, connected people — *"not-exactly-meritocratic ripple effects."*
Worth knowing so a low score reads as a system feature, not a personal failing.

### Jeremy Schifeling — *Career Coach GPT* ⭐ most operational
The most directly actionable. Contributes:

- **Holy Trinity** — Headline, Location, Photo. The three things recruiters always see when a profile
  surfaces. If these three fail, nothing else is evaluated.
- **The headline is the most heavily weighted field** — *"its very brevity (limited to just 220
  characters) makes it the hardest part of your profile to game. And so they've assigned it extra
  importance."* The mechanism is what makes this credible, not the assertion.
- **Location is aspirational, not factual** — *"update your LinkedIn Location to where you want to be
  found, not where you're sitting right now."*
- **The 50% Rule** — *"50% qualified candidates have the best chance of getting interviews, and your
  chances actually decrease beyond that."* Deeply counterintuitive and behaviorally important: a
  profile engineered to look 100% qualified reads as overqualified or fabricated.
- **The 3 Ns** — every bullet carries a **N**umber, a **N**ame, or a **N**otable.
- **Top Third Rule** — recruiters read the top third; front-load.
- **The InMail responsiveness signal** — LinkedIn ranks up users who answer InMails fast and ranks
  down those who don't, because recruiter InMail credits are scarce. **No other source mentions
  this, and it is entirely invisible to profile inspection.** It means an unanswered InMail is not
  neutral — it is a ranking penalty.
- **Two SEO plays for one** — an optimized profile also ranks on Google.

### Steve Dalton — *The Job Closer* (ch. 3) ⭐ the contrarian
The dissenting voice, and valuable precisely for that. Dalton's position: **"objectively perfect,
subjectively good enough."**

- *"What employers are typically looking for on LinkedIn is your* objective *experience: job titles
  held, employers worked for, schools attended, certifications and degrees attained."*
- *"Aspirational headlines like 'Seeking new opportunity in product management' don't effectively
  convey confidence."* Prefers a broad professional description in **≤15 words**.
- **Explicitly warns against over-investment**: *"don't fall into the quagmire of trying to create new
  and original content for LinkedIn."* Three acceptable levels of effort — objective info only,
  select resume bullets, or full replication.
- The chapter checklist is four items. **Error-free is #1.**

**Why this matters:** Dalton's is the anti-perfectionism position, and it is the correct
counterweight to the most common failure — polishing instead of shipping. When the rubric and
Dalton conflict, Dalton is the tiebreaker on *how much time to spend*, even where *Linked* wins on
*what to write*.

### Madeline Mann — *Reverse the Search*
Contributes the positioning discipline:

- **Value-add over history** — lead with the value you offer, not where you've been.
- **Translate internal titles** into market-recognizable ones.
- **Keyword construction**: top-5 keywords from the FLORY/GLORY exercise + title keywords → a
  combined top-5 for the profile top.
- **Career pivoting rule** — *"focus on the role you want in the future, not the roles you've had in
  the past."* The single clearest statement of the career-changer principle.
- **Network scale: 2,000+** connections for someone 10+ years in.
- **Brand refresh cadence: every 6–8 weeks** — a calendar event, not a mood. This is the structural
  fix for staleness.

### Phyl Terry — *Never Search Alone*
Contributes the **anchor**: Candidate–Market Fit. Nothing on the profile is scored in the abstract —
every line is scored against "the problem you solve and who you solve it for." Also contributes the
**LinkedIn Rehab group exercise** (peer review with a Job Search Council) and the CMF announcement
post. The JSC is the accountability structure; the profile work is meant to be *social*, not solo.

### Brossman, Gower, Rose & O'Connor — *Job Hunting in the AI Era*
The only source written for the AI-screening era. Contributes: LinkedIn as **digital first
impression**; publishing cadence as a visibility lever; Persona creation; the Shape Profile (I/T/Pi/X)
for describing depth-vs-breadth; and the "give first" posture for engagement (share, connect,
encourage, ask better questions).

### Abed & Altucher — *Fire Me I Beg You*
Contributes **social proof as a precondition, not a bonus**: *"having an excellent LinkedIn profile
and having social proof are the biggest factors in both getting an interview and nailing it."* Also:
narrative consistency (*"job titles, descriptions, summary and even your recommendations all telling
the same story"*), first-person voice, buzzword elimination, and the blunt instruction to **pay for a
professional headshot**. Their social-proof list sets a bar for what counts: press mentions, 10+
comments on a post, conference speaking, **20+ recommendations**.

### Reid Hoffman & Ben Casnocha — *The Start-Up of You*
Contributes **professional identity independent of employer** — the philosophical basis for the custom
URL, the Featured section, and publishing. Also ABZ planning and permanent beta: the profile is never
"done," it's versioned. Argues the network is an asset to be deliberately built, not a byproduct.

### Dawn Graham — *Switchers* 🆕 (added 2026-08-05)
**The most relevant source for a career-changer, and absent from v1.** Core mechanism: hiring is
pattern-matching, and **similarity attracts** — the reader hires the candidate who most resembles
their mental template. A switcher's profile must therefore do the translation work *for* the reader
rather than leaving a gap for them to bridge. Practical implications: lead with the target-role frame
and let history support it; name the bridge asset explicitly; never make the hiring manager infer the
relevance of prior industry experience.

### Dorie Clark — *Stand Out* / *Reinventing You* 🆕 (added 2026-08-05)
Contributes the **known-for** test: what is the one idea this person is associated with? Distinguishes
*being hireable* from *being sought out*. Where every other source optimizes the profile for a
recruiter running a search, Clark asks whether there's any reason to follow this person absent an open
role. Feeds the Featured, activity, and About-differentiation criteria.

### Carla Harris — *Expect to Win* 🆕 (added 2026-08-05)
Contributes **performance currency vs. relationship currency**. Performance currency is what you
delivered — it's what bullets and the 3 Ns capture. Relationship currency is who will vouch — it's
what recommendations, endorsements, and visible advocates capture. Harris's argument is that
performance currency has *diminishing* returns past a point and relationship currency has
*increasing* ones. On a LinkedIn profile this maps precisely: a fifth quantified bullet is worth less
than a first recent manager recommendation. Also contributes the **sponsor** concept — is there anyone
visible on this profile who would spend capital on this person?

### Richard Bolles — *What Color Is Your Parachute?*
Contributes the **transferable-skills inventory** (Flower Exercise) — the raw material for translating
prior-industry experience into target vocabulary. The discipline: name skills in the *market's* words,
not the prior industry's.

### Jenny Blake — *Pivot*
Contributes the **one-degree pivot**: a move the market can believe because it's adjacent, not a leap.
Diagnostic: is the profile asking a reader to accept one change of variable, or several at once?
(Example: industry change + function continuity = one degree. Good. Adding a title change *and* an
industry change *and* a level change would not be.)

### Cal Newport — *So Good They Can't Ignore You* / *Deep Work*
The **counterweight**. Newport's career-capital thesis argues against exactly the language most
LinkedIn advice produces: passion statements, mission language, "driven by." His position is that rare
and valuable skills are the currency and that asserting passion is what people do when they lack
proof. **Use Newport as an editing filter, not a generator:** after drafting, cut every sentence that
asserts enthusiasm and keep every sentence that demonstrates capability.

### Steve Dalton — *The 2-Hour Job Search*
Contributes **LAMP** for generating the target-company list that feeds the keyword harvest. Not a
profile framework, but the profile can't be targeted without the list.

### The Job InSiders — *LinkedIn Profile Grader* / Recruiter Cheatsheet
Former LinkedIn employees. Contributes the documented **recruiter filter order**, which is what
justifies the rubric's dimension weights rather than leaving them arbitrary.

**Deliberately excluded:** Marc Cosentino (*Case in Point*) — case-interview methodology has no
LinkedIn-profile implication. Recorded so the omission reads as a decision.

## 3. Where the sources genuinely disagree

Documented rather than resolved. A methodology that hides its conflicts is more confident and less
honest.

### 3.1 Headline posture — aspirational vs. objective ⚠️ THE contested criterion

| Position | Sources | Claim | Rests on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aspirational** | *Linked*, *Career Coach GPT*, *Reverse the Search* | Put the **desired** title in the headline. "Seeking"/"Aspiring [role]" is acceptable. Mann: focus on the role you want, not the roles you've had. | The algorithm can only match tokens that are present. |
| **Objective** | *The Job Closer* | Aspirational headlines *"don't effectively convey confidence."* Use current title + employer, ≤15 words. | Recruiters are risk-averse and read hedging as weakness. |

**Synthesis (the default when the user expresses no preference):** lead with the most target-adjacent
title claimable **truthfully**, with no hedging language ("aspiring," "seeking," "future"). This
satisfies the algorithmic constraint (token present) and Dalton's objection (no hedge). Always state
in the report which way it was scored.

### 3.2 How much effort a profile deserves
Dalton says *objectively perfect, subjectively good enough* and explicitly warns against creating
original content. *Linked* and *Career Coach GPT* treat the About section as a replacement for the
cover letter — i.e. worth real writing effort. **Unresolved.** The practical read: Dalton is right
about *marginal* effort past a floor; the others are right that the floor is higher than "objective
info only" when you're changing industries. A career-changer cannot rely on objective information,
because their objective information points at the wrong job.

### 3.3 Keyword density vs. semantic depth ⚠️ books are out of date here
*Career Coach GPT*: *"the LinkedIn algorithm isn't only interested in matching the desired skills — it
also wants skill* density." This was true when written and is **now actively wrong** — see §4.

### 3.4 Open to Work — public badge vs. recruiters-only
See §4.4. Genuinely contested *in the field*, not just between books.

### 3.5 Recommendation volume
Abed & Altucher set the social-proof bar at **20+ recommendations**. Every other source, and the v1
rubric, treats 3+ recent and relevant as sufficient. The 20+ figure appears to be a
thought-leadership bar rather than a hiring bar. **Rubric follows the 3+ standard; the 20+ figure is
noted, not adopted.**

## 4. 2026 platform behavior

> **Adoption-delay discount — read before using anything in this section.**
> New LinkedIn functionality is not automatically relevant to a given job search. The gap between
> *shipped* and *actually used by the recruiters who will see your profile* is large and measurable:
> **the typical LinkedIn Recruiter seat now bundles 24+ distinct AI capabilities, and most teams touch
> only two or three of them.** Practitioner sentiment is evidence, not noise — a recruiter who says
> they hate a feature is telling you the real adoption curve. Weight platform changes by *observed
> recruiter behavior*, not by announcement.

### 4.1 Semantic matching replaced keyword matching ⭐ the biggest change
*Verified 2026-08-05.* LinkedIn has moved from keyword search to **semantic entity mapping** — a
Knowledge Graph that maps relationships between skills, titles, companies, and industries, with an
LLM-based matching engine ranking candidates by semantic relevance. AI-powered recruiter search
formally ranks by semantic relevance as of the **May 2026** rollout.

**Consequences, in order of importance:**
1. **Keyword stuffing now actively degrades match quality.** It is no longer merely bad writing.
2. **Prose that demonstrates a capability beats a list that names it.** A term used in a sentence that
   shows the work substantially outperforms the same term sitting in a skills list.
3. **Related-term coverage beats repetition.** "If you list Python but never Pandas or NumPy, it reads
   as shallow." Cover the *cluster*, don't repeat the *token*.
4. This is **the single largest departure from all nine books**, every one of which optimizes for term
   density.

⚠️ **Note a conflict in the 2026 sources themselves:** one 2026 audit source recorded a "Q1 2026
update raised keyword-density weight in Headline + About," which points the opposite direction from
the semantic-matching finding. Both can be partly true (the headline may remain literally indexed
while the body is semantically evaluated), but this is **unresolved and should be re-verified at the
November refresh.** Do not treat either as settled.

### 4.2 AI-assisted sourcing compresses the funnel
*Verified 2026-08-05.* LinkedIn's **Hiring Assistant** (GA end of September 2025) builds sourcing
strategies from role context rather than Boolean strings. LinkedIn's own charter-customer data:
recruiters reviewed **62% fewer profiles**, saved 4+ hours per role, and saw 69% higher InMail
acceptance. Agentic hiring products reached ~**$450M annualized run-rate by April 2026**. February
2026's quarterly drop added Teams collaboration, AI Follow-Ups, AI Applicant Targeting, and **Verified
Applicant Spotlight**.

**Consequence:** fewer profiles get human eyes, so semantic fit of the ones that surface matters more.
**Counter-consequence (the adoption discount):** Hiring Assistant evaluates skills from self-reported,
unverified profile data, and most buyers cannot tell whether it surfaces *better* candidates or merely
*faster* ones. Enterprise adoption ≠ the recruiter working your specific req.

### 4.3 Activity is a ranking input, not just a vanity metric
*Verified 2026-08-05.* Recency of activity and connection distance are explicit ranking factors.
**Profiles that go dark for months lose search visibility even when every field is perfectly
optimized.** Incomplete sections are demoted. Teaching/insight posts are surfaced; promotional posts
are suppressed. LinkedIn's own figure, surfaced in profile analytics: posting ≥1×/week correlates with
up to **4× profile views**.

The InMail-responsiveness signal from *Career Coach GPT* belongs here too — it's an activity signal
that is invisible on the profile itself.

### 4.4 Open to Work — the data and the backlash both hold
*Verified 2026-08-05.* Genuinely contested, and the best live example of why the adoption discount
matters.

- **LinkedIn's data:** members with the public frame are ~**2× as likely** to get a recruiter message
  and find jobs **>20% faster**; the banner raises InMail response rates up to **40%**.
- **The backlash:** in July 2026 an engineer's post — *"never use the green Open to Work badge…
  negative signal for hiring managers and recruiters"* — drew **1M+ views** and hundreds of comments.
  The "air of desperation" read is widespread among hiring managers even though the platform data
  contradicts it.
- **Resolution:** **recruiters-only visibility** captures nearly all the algorithmic benefit without
  the public signal. This is the default recommendation for anyone employed or ambivalent. For the
  recently laid off, sources report no meaningful stigma in 2026 and the public banner signals
  availability.
- **Scoring rule: recruiters-only is NOT penalized.** Score visibility scope on intent, not on
  bravery.

### 4.5 Verification badge
*Verified 2026-08-05.* Free identity verification, and **Verified Applicant Spotlight** shipped in the
Feb 2026 Recruiter update — meaning verification is now surfaced *to recruiters as a filter/signal*,
not just displayed on the profile. Low effort, rising value. Treat as a cheap point.

### 4.6 Character limits — books are stale, use these

| Field | Books say | **Actual (verified 2026-08-05)** | Visible before cutoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline | 120 (*Linked*) / 220 (*CCG*) | **220** | ~70 in mobile search results |
| About | 2,000 (*Linked*, *CCG*) | **2,600** | **~300 desktop / ~200 mobile** |
| Experience description | — | **2,000** | ~250 |
| Experience position title | — | **100** | — |
| Post | — | **3,000** | ~200–210 |
| Comment | — | **1,250** | — |
| Connection request note | — | **300** | — |
| InMail body | — | **2,000** | — |
| Skills | — | **50 max** | Top 3 pinned |
| Custom URL slug | — | 3–100 | — |

**Engagement sweet spots:** About ~1,800–2,200 characters. Posts 1,800–2,100 characters — note that
LinkedIn is unusual here; longer outperforms shorter, opposite to most platforms.

## 5. Synthesis — what actually drives the score

Reconciling all of the above into the model the rubric implements.

| Dimension | Weight | The question | Primary sources |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **A. Discoverability** | 32 | Do you appear in the search at all? | Schifeling (Job Title/Location/Skills), *Linked*, Job InSiders |
| **B. Relevance & Semantic Depth** | 26 | Once found, do you match? | 2026 semantic matching, Mann, Graham |
| **C. Credibility & Proof** | 22 | Is the claim believable? | 3 Ns, Harris (both currencies), Abed & Altucher |
| **D. Human Filter** | 12 | Does a tired human click? | Dalton (error-free floor), Holy Trinity |
| **E. Engagement Signals** | 8 | Are you worth an InMail? | 2026 activity ranking, Hoffman, Clark |

**Why Discoverability is weighted highest:** it's a gate, not a gradient (§1.1). Everything downstream
multiplies by zero if this fails.

**Why the weights are a prior, not a measurement:** they derive from documented recruiter filter order
and the gate argument, not from outcome data. **Treat a 4-point difference between two profiles as
noise.** This caveat must survive into every report.

### The reliable failure modes, ranked
Observed across real audits and consistent with what the books predict:

1. **Empty most-recent role.** The highest-frequency catastrophic gap. Skill tags render as an empty
   role to a recruiter, and 2026 demotes incomplete sections outright.
2. **About section opens with personality instead of positioning.** The ~300 visible characters are
   the entire section for most readers, and nearly everyone wastes them.
3. **Location set to where you live rather than where you want to be found.**
4. **Top-3 pinned skills chosen by habit rather than by what a recruiter filters on first.**
5. **Recommendations stale or absent** — the relationship-currency gap, and the cheapest large win
   available to most people.
6. **Prior roles left as historical artifacts** rather than translated toward the target.

### The three fixes that almost always dominate
Rank by **points recoverable per minute**, never by lowest raw score. A 0 on a 2-point criterion is
cosmetic; a 2 on a 10-point criterion is the whole problem. Empirically, for a mid-career
career-changer, the top three are nearly always: **(1) bullets on the most recent role, (2) the About
billboard, (3) the headline title token.** Everything else is second-order.

## 6. Applying this to the user

Standing positioning comes from `job-search-profile.md` §1 (targets, strengths, proof points) and, if
present, the user's two-pager / Candidate–Market Fit statement from the discovery module. Derive from it:
- **Topic DNA** — 2–3 pillars that must appear consistently across headline, About, and posts.
- **Strengths to lead with** — from the profile, not inferred.
- **Location** — LinkedIn location is a binary search filter; check it against where the user wants to
  be found, not where they live, and flag any mismatch.
- **Follow-through constraint** — every audit ends with a stop rule and a points-per-minute ranking so
  the list is finishable; the re-eval loop closes the accountability gap; Mann's 6–8 week brand refresh
  becomes a recurring task, not an intention.

## 7. Prompt library (from *Career Coach GPT*, ch. 2)

Kept verbatim from the book notes — the direct ancestors of the rewrite playbook.

- **Headline:** *Generate a 220 character LinkedIn profile Headline based on the following template,
  desired job title, and resume. Template: DESIRED JOB (list "Seeking" if the candidate lacks
  experience) | RELEVANT SKILLS FOR JOB*
- **Top Skills:** *What are the 20 most common skills listed on job descriptions for JOB TITLE?*
- **About:** *Generate a [2,600] character LinkedIn About section… Start with an opening sentence that
  states the candidate's focus on their desired job title. ► three relevant resume bullets ►
  Specialties: most relevant skills*
- **Recommendation request:** *Generate a LinkedIn Recommendation request for my former boss/client,
  X. Friendly, grateful, respectful. Under 500 characters.*
- **Cover photo:** *Please generate 10 ideas for a photo of X role in action.*
- **Learn skills fast:** *What are the specific fastest and cheapest ways to learn SKILL?*

⚠️ **The headline and About prompts as written are pre-semantic-matching.** The "Seeking" instruction
conflicts with Dalton and with §3.1's synthesis; the 2,000-character figure is stale. Use them as
structure, not as literal instructions.

**Tools referenced:** `snappr.com/photo-analyzer` (free AI photo analysis, pulls straight from
LinkedIn) · `linkedin.com/interview-prep` · `thejobinsiders.com` (LinkedIn Profile Grader).

## 8. Known limitations of this methodology

1. **Weights are reasoned, not validated.** A defensible prior, not a measurement.
2. **No live LinkedIn access.** Cannot verify actual search ranking or impressions; analytics must be
   supplied manually.
3. **US/English-centric.** Photo and title conventions don't transfer to markets where photos are
   discouraged or prohibited.
4. **Platform behavior moves fast and the sources conflict** (§4.1). Re-verify before relying on any
   §4 claim after 2026-11-05.
5. **Optimizes for inbound recruiter discovery** — not the only or best channel (§1.5).
6. **Books lag the platform by 2–4 years** on mechanics while remaining sound on principles. Where
   they conflict with verified platform behavior, platform wins; where they conflict on *judgment*
   (how much effort, what posture), the conflict is real and gets surfaced, not resolved.

## Refresh protocol (runs at the start of a score if this file is >90 days old)

1. Run 2–4 web searches for the current year: "LinkedIn algorithm <year> recruiter search", "LinkedIn
   profile character limits <year>", "LinkedIn Hiring Assistant / AI sourcing <year>", "Open to Work
   badge recruiters <year>".
2. Compare against §4. Only change a claim when a dated, credible source contradicts it.
3. If you can write files: update §4 claims with `Verified YYYY-MM-DD`, bump **Swept**, add a Changelog
   row. If you can't (e.g. a read-only skill), list the deltas at the top of the report as
   "2026→<year> platform updates applied this run" and score with them.
4. No web access → score with this file as-is and state its sweep date in the report.
