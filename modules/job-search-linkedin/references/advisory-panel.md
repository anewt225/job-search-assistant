# The Advisory Panel

**Layer 2 of the assessment. Unscored, qualitative, and delivered after the composite.**

The 100-point rubric answers *how good is this profile*. The panel answers *what would each of these
people say if they read it* — which catches what a score cannot: posture, positioning, whether the
writing sounds like a human, and whether the whole thing is aimed at the right target.

Seven advisors. Each brings their **actual frameworks**, not a single question — apply the framework,
then deliver the verdict.

---

## How to run the panel

1. **Run it after the score, before the rewrites.** The panel's job is to explain *why* the score is
   what it is, and to set up the rewrite direction.
2. **Each advisor gets 2–4 sentences.** Not a paragraph. If an advisor has nothing distinctive to
   say about this profile, say so in one line rather than padding — a panel where everyone always
   has a strong opinion is decorative.
3. **Write in the advisor's own logic, not their voice.** Do not impersonate, do not use first
   person, do not put words in their mouths. "Dalton's framework would flag X" — not "Steve says…"
4. **Disagreement is the point.** Where two advisors would give opposite advice on the same profile,
   say so explicitly. Dalton and Mann will conflict on almost every career-changer profile. That
   conflict is information.
5. **Every verdict names the artifact.** Quote the actual headline, the actual opening line. A panel
   verdict that could apply to any profile is worthless.
6. **Close with the panel's collective read** — one sentence naming what all seven are circling.

## 1. Steve Dalton — *The Job Closer*, *The 2-Hour Job Search*

**Role on the panel:** the contrarian, and the anti-perfectionism check.

### Frameworks to apply

**"Objectively perfect, subjectively good enough."** Dalton's governing standard. The objective layer
— job titles, employers, dates, schools, certifications — must be flawless. The subjective layer —
narrative, voice, positioning — needs only to be adequate. He explicitly warns against *"the quagmire
of trying to create new and original content for LinkedIn."*

**The three effort tiers.** Dalton offers a genuine choice, and the tier should be a conscious
selection rather than a default:
1. Objective information only (employers, titles, locations, dates)
2. Objective information plus *select* resume bullets — best bullet first
3. Full resume replication, using identical language to avoid double work

**Error-free as a floor condition.** Item #1 on his chapter checklist. Recruiters must reject 99%+ of
applicants; a typo, a broken date, a contradictory title is a free reason to say No. This is the one
place where absence of defect outweighs presence of brilliance.

**The ≤15-word headline.** A broad description of you as a professional — one phrase or a collection
of descriptors. Explicitly rejects aspirational framing: *"aspirational headlines like 'Seeking new
opportunity in product management' don't effectively convey confidence."*

**The 6-second scan.** Whatever survives six seconds is the profile.

### Panel questions
- What here is a free reason to say No?
- Which effort tier is this profile actually at, and is that a decision or an accident?
- Does the meaning survive a 6-second scan?
- Is any hedging language ("aspiring," "seeking," "transitioning") costing more confidence than the keyword buys?

### Reads as failing
Any typo or date inconsistency. A headline over ~15 words. Hedging language. Also — and this is the
Dalton-specific failure the other advisors miss — **a profile that has been rewritten five times and
published zero times.** Over-investment is a failure mode in his framework, not a virtue.

### Informs criteria
A1, A2 (headline posture and length) · D3 (error-free, narrative consistency) · effort-allocation
across the whole action plan

## 2. Madeline Mann — *Reverse the Search*

**Role on the panel:** the positioning discipline, and the career-pivot authority.

### Frameworks to apply

**Value-add over previous experience.** The organizing principle of her Personal Brand chapter. A
profile should lead with *the value you offer*, not a chronology of where you've been. Most profiles
invert this and read as a résumé with a photo.

**"Focus on the role you want in the future, not the roles you've had in the past."** Her clearest
statement of the pivot rule, and the direct counterweight to Dalton's objective-information position.
For a career-changer, objective information points at the wrong job.

**Internal-title translation.** Rename internal or invented titles to market-recognizable equivalents.
A title only you and your former employer understand is invisible to search and confusing to humans.

**The keyword construction method.** Take the top 5 keywords from the GLORY/FLORY exercise, combine
with the target *title* keywords, and derive a new top-5 for the profile. Those five belong at the
**top** of the profile, the résumé, and the personal site. Additional role-relevant and
industry-specific keywords go in the body.

**Be explicit about company type and industry** — *"because you appear as a perfect fit to employers
in that industry."* Vagueness about the target industry is not optionality, it's invisibility.

**Network scale: 2,000+** for someone 10+ years into a career.

**The 6–8 week brand refresh.** A recurring calendar event: update anything stale, add new contacts,
reach out to five people you've lost touch with. Structural, not motivational.

**"Measure your progress in interactions, not applications."** Context for the whole exercise — the
profile serves the conversations, it does not replace them.

### Panel questions
- Does this profile lead with value offered, or with places been?
- Are the top-5 keywords actually at the top, or buried?
- Is any internal/invented title left untranslated?
- Is the target industry stated plainly enough that an employer in it sees a perfect fit?
- Is there a refresh cadence, or is this profile refreshed only in a panic?

### Reads as failing
A chronological About section. Target industry inferable but never named. Internal titles left raw.
Network well under 2,000 for a 10+ year career. No refresh mechanism.

### Informs criteria
A4 (skills selection) · B1, B2 (About: value-first construction) · B4 (prior-role translation) ·
B5 (keyword coverage) · E1 (network scale)

## 3. Dawn Graham — *Switchers*

**Role on the panel:** the career-changer authority. **The most important advisor for a profile that
is changing industry, function, or level.**

### Frameworks to apply

**Similarity attracts.** Hiring is pattern-matching. The reader hires whoever most resembles the
mental template they're holding. This is not a bias to be scolded — it's the mechanism the profile
must work with.

**The translation burden must sit with the writer.** A switcher's profile fails when it presents raw
prior-industry experience and leaves the hiring manager to work out the relevance. They will not do
that work; they have 40 other reqs. **Every unit of inference you require is a unit of risk you
transfer to the reader.**

**Lead with the target frame, let history support it.** The order matters as much as the content. The
same facts arranged target-first read as a qualified candidate; arranged chronologically they read as
someone who used to do something else.

**Name the bridge asset.** The specific thing that makes the switch credible — a certification, a
project, an adjacent responsibility, a domain overlap. Unnamed, the switch looks like a leap.

**Address the obvious objection before it's raised.** A switcher's profile should defuse the "but they
have no experience in X" reaction rather than hoping it doesn't occur.

### Panel questions
- Who is doing the translation work here — the profile, or the reader?
- What is the bridge asset, and is it named in the first screenful?
- What's the obvious objection, and where does the profile answer it?
- Rearranged target-first, would the same facts read differently?

### Reads as failing
Prior-industry roles described in prior-industry vocabulary. A hospitality role that reads as
restaurant duties rather than as operations, systems implementation, and training at scale. A profile
where the target role appears for the first time below the fold.

### Informs criteria
B1 (billboard framing) · B4 (prior-role translation — **the highest-stakes criterion for a switcher**) ·
A1 (headline posture) · the entire rewrite direction

## 4. Carla Harris — *Expect to Win*

**Role on the panel:** the proof-and-advocacy auditor.

### Frameworks to apply

**Performance currency vs. relationship currency.** Performance currency is what you delivered —
captured by quantified bullets and the 3 Ns. Relationship currency is who will vouch for you —
captured by recommendations, endorsements, and visible advocates. Harris's core argument:
**performance currency has diminishing returns past a threshold; relationship currency has increasing
ones.** On a profile this maps exactly — the fifth quantified bullet is worth less than the first
recent manager recommendation.

**Sponsor vs. mentor.** A mentor advises you; a sponsor spends their own capital on you. Ask of the
profile: is there anyone visible here who would spend capital? Three recommendations from 2016 peers
show no sponsor. One recent recommendation from a senior person in the target domain shows one.

**Perception is the co-worker you never asked for.** How you are read is a fact about your career
whether or not it's fair. The profile is the highest-leverage place to manage it deliberately.

**Ask for what you want, explicitly.** Recruiters do not infer the ask. The call to action is not
optional politeness — it's the mechanism.

### Panel questions
- Where does performance currency stop paying and relationship currency need to start?
- Is there a visible sponsor — anyone senior, recent, and in-domain?
- What does this profile's perception gap look like: what will a reader conclude that isn't true?
- Is the ask stated, with a way to act on it?

### Reads as failing
Heavy quantified bullets paired with stale or peer-only recommendations — the classic imbalance, and
the most common one in a technically-strong profile. No CTA. No contact method for a recruiter without
InMail credits.

### Informs criteria
C1 (quantified achievement — where it saturates) · C2 (recommendations — **usually the cheapest large
win on the board**) · B2 (About CTA)

## 5. Dorie Clark — *Stand Out*, *Reinventing You*

**Role on the panel:** the differentiation test.

### Frameworks to apply

**The known-for test.** What is the one idea this person is associated with? Not their job title —
their idea. Most profiles establish what someone *is* and never what they *think*.

**Hireable vs. sought-out.** Every other advisor optimizes the profile for a recruiter running a
search. Clark asks the orthogonal question: absent any open role, is there a reason to follow this
person? That's the difference between being found when someone looks and being remembered when they
don't.

**Reinvention requires proof, not announcement.** You cannot declare a new positioning; you have to
show evidence of it. A Featured section with actual work is proof. A headline claiming a new domain
with nothing behind it is announcement.

**Build the body of work in public.** Writing, speaking, and projects are what convert expertise into
recognized expertise. This is the long game the profile either supports or doesn't.

### Panel questions
- What is this person known for — in one phrase, from the profile alone?
- Is there any reason to follow them if you're not hiring?
- Where's the proof of the new positioning, as distinct from the claim of it?
- What do they say that a hundred similar profiles don't?

### Reads as failing
A profile that is competent, complete, well-keyworded, and **interchangeable** with every peer in the
target role. Convergence on peer language makes you findable and forgettable. Also: a claimed pivot
with an empty Featured section.

### Informs criteria
B1 (billboard point-of-view) · C3 (Featured/work samples) · E2 (activity, original content) ·
the peer-differentiation half of the harvest

## 6. Cal Newport — *So Good They Can't Ignore You*, *Deep Work*

**Role on the panel:** the counterweight and the editing filter.

### Frameworks to apply

**Career capital over passion.** Rare and valuable skills are the currency. The "follow your passion"
frame is not merely soft — Newport argues it's actively misleading, and that asserting passion is
what people do when they lack proof. **This cuts directly against the language most LinkedIn advice
generates.**

**The craftsman mindset.** Focus on what you can offer the world, not what the world can offer you.
An About section written in craftsman mode reads as evidence; written in passion mode it reads as a
personal statement.

**Use as a subtractive filter, not a generator.** After drafting: delete every sentence that *asserts*
enthusiasm, mission, or drive. Keep every sentence that *demonstrates* capability. Whatever survives
is the real profile. If very little survives, that's the finding.

**Deep work as a differentiator.** In a field where most output is shallow, evidence of sustained
difficult work is itself a signal. Look for whether the profile shows anything that took real time.

### Panel questions
- Strike every line that asserts passion, drive, or mission. What's left?
- Is capability demonstrated, or claimed?
- What here required rare skill, and is that legible to an outsider?
- Which adjectives are doing work that a number should be doing?

### Reads as failing
"Passionate about…" · "Driven by…" · "I love solving hard problems" · any sentence about how the
person feels regarding their work rather than what the work produced. Also, an About section that
survives the filter at under 30% of its original length.

### Informs criteria
B2 (About body — adjective walls are capped at 1) · C1 (quantified achievement) · the rewrite voice
rules

## 7. Phyl Terry — *Never Search Alone*

**Role on the panel:** the anchor, and the accountability structure. **Runs last — Terry's verdict
frames the others.**

### Frameworks to apply

**Candidate–Market Fit.** The anchor for everything. CMF states the problem you solve and who you
solve it for. **Nothing on the profile is evaluated in the abstract** — every line is measured against
the CMF, and a brilliant line that doesn't serve it is a liability, not a bonus.

**The consultative approach.** Frame around client need and value-add rather than personal
credentials. The profile answers "what do you need?" before "here's what I have."

**The LinkedIn Rehab group exercise.** Terry's method is explicitly *social* — profile review happens
with a Job Search Council, not alone. Peers catch what the writer cannot see, because the writer
knows what they meant. **A solo audit, including this one, is a substitute for the group exercise and
should say so.**

**The CMF announcement post.** Stating the CMF publicly converts a private intention into a commitment
others can act on — and is itself a profile artifact.

**Job Search Council as accountability.** The structural answer to profile work that never ships. The
deadline is external because internal deadlines don't hold.

### Panel questions
- Is every section traceable to the CMF? Name any line that isn't.
- Does the profile lead with client need or with personal credentials?
- Has this been reviewed by anyone other than the person who wrote it?
- What is the external deadline on the recommended changes, and who else knows about it?

### Reads as failing
A profile that is internally excellent but aimed at a target the CMF doesn't name. Any section
optimizing for a role the person has decided against. And — Terry's distinctive catch — **a
recommendation list with no external accountability attached**, which is the state in which profile
work reliably fails to ship.

### Informs criteria
Every criterion (CMF is the anchor for all scoring) · the action plan's deadline and stop-rule
structure

## Panel output format

```markdown
## Advisory Panel

*Seven readers, seven frameworks. Unscored — this explains the score rather than adding to it.*

**Dalton** — [2–4 sentences. Free reasons to say No. Effort tier. Hedging.]

**Mann** — [Value-first vs. chronology. Keyword placement. Industry named?]

**Graham** — [Who carries the translation burden. The bridge asset. The unaddressed objection.]

**Harris** — [Performance vs. relationship currency balance. Sponsor visible? The ask.]

**Clark** — [Known-for, in one phrase. Interchangeable or distinctive. Proof vs. announcement.]

**Newport** — [What survives the passion filter. Demonstrated vs. claimed.]

**Terry** — [CMF traceability. Reviewed by anyone? External deadline?]

---

**Where the panel splits:** [Name at least one genuine disagreement and what it turns on.
Dalton vs. Mann on headline posture is the usual one for a career-changer.]

**What they're all circling:** [One sentence. The thing seven different frameworks independently
land on. This is usually the real finding.]
```

---

## Discipline rules

- **No impersonation.** Apply the framework, attribute the reasoning, never fake the voice.
- **No unanimity theater.** If six advisors say the same thing, compress them and give the space to
  the one who disagrees.
- **Quote the artifact.** Every verdict cites actual profile text.
- **The panel does not change the score.** If a panel finding is severe enough to matter numerically,
  that's evidence the rubric is missing a criterion — log it for the next methodology refresh rather
  than adjusting the composite by feel.
- **An advisor with nothing to say says so.** One line: "Nothing distinctive to flag here." That is a
  real and useful verdict.
